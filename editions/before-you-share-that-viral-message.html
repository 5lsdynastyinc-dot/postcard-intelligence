Before You Share That Viral Message: Five Checks That Can Change the Story

A confident headline is a starting point. Here’s how to look for the source, the context and what remains uncertain.

POSTCARD · 26 October 2026 · About 4 minutes · Edition PC-INFO-20261026-01

A message lands in your group chat. It sounds urgent. Someone you trust has forwarded it, and the comments are already filling up.

Before passing it on, ask a smaller question than “Do I believe this?” Ask: “What exactly can I establish?”

You do not need to settle every disagreement on the internet. A few careful checks can help you decide whether to share, investigate further or leave a claim alone. They are a starting point, not a guarantee that a message is true.

1. Find the person or organisation behind the claim

Start with the original statement, document or recording, rather than the account that forwarded it. The House of Commons Library describes tracing claims to their origins and examining context as central parts of fact-checking. [1]

If a post attributes an announcement to an organisation, look for that announcement on the organisation's own channels. If it cites research, try to find the actual study.

When the trail stops at “someone said”, keep that gap visible. Being unable to find the original does not, by itself, prove the message false. It means you have not yet established where it came from.

2. Check the date and the place

WHO's public information guidance encourages readers to examine dates and ask whether material has been taken out of context. [3]

Imagine an old notice being forwarded without its date. That is a hypothetical example, but it shows why the date can change what a reader reasonably concludes.

Also ask where the message applies. Before treating a rule or announcement as relevant to you, establish the country, institution or area actually covered. That is our practical recommendation for checking scope, not a claim about any particular current rule.

3. Open the evidence, not just the headline

Follow the links. Read the relevant passage and the surrounding explanation. Does it support what the message says, or only something narrower?

The Commons Library's fact-checking guidance describes examining which statements can be verified and checking whether other sources confirm or contradict them. [1]

For example, “researchers are investigating a possibility” and “a result has been established” are different claims. This is an illustrative distinction, not a report of a particular study.

If the evidence is unavailable, say so. Do not fill the missing passage with a guess.

4. Look behind the picture—and behind repeated reports

The Commons Library notes that genuine pictures can be shared in the wrong context. Finding an earlier version through reverse image search can help investigate where an image came from and whether its caption fits. [2]

An unsuccessful search does not establish authenticity. The same guidance also warns against relying on visual oddities alone to decide whether something was generated by AI. [2]

For written reports, follow their references too. If several pages point back to one announcement, our editorial recommendation is to treat them as repetitions of that announcement, rather than assume they independently confirmed it.

5. Make room for “not established yet”

Sometimes the honest conclusion is that a claim remains unresolved. The Commons Library's image-verification guidance explicitly recognises uncertainty and the possibility that knowledge will be revised. [2]

We suggest choosing among three practical responses: share with the necessary context; investigate further; or hold the message until better evidence is available. These are POSTCARD's proposed reader actions, not an official rating system.

You can also ask the sender: “Please share the original source and date.” That keeps the conversation focused on evidence rather than on the person's motives.

Before you forward the next urgent message, give yourself permission to pause. Useful information deserves attention. Uncertainty deserves an honest label.
